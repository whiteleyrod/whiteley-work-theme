Shoulder rotational ROM · a clinical reasoning tool

The arc doesn’t shrink.
It shifts.

In an uninjured shoulder, both arms are assumed to share roughly the same total rotational range of motion (TRROM) — internal plus external rotation combined. A side‑to‑side difference in humeral torsion doesn’t shrink that arc; it slides it toward more external or more internal rotation. Read internal rotation alone (“GIRD”), and a normal bony shift can look exactly like a soft‑tissue problem — or hide a real one.

The assumption underneath every ROM chart

Passive shoulder rotation isn’t stopped by bone hitting bone — it’s set by capsule and soft‑tissue extensibility. Absent injury or adaptation, one arm’s total rotational arc should look much like the other’s, even though that person’s own arc may be quite different in size to someone else’s. What differs between a throwing arm and its resting partner isn’t usually how much rotation is available — it’s where that rotation is centred, because the humerus itself is twisted by a different amount on each side.

Left panel

When torsion is equal side‑to‑side, equal soft‑tissue extensibility produces aligned arcs — IR and ER match on both arms.

Right panel

With a typical ~12° more retrotorsion on the throwing side, the involved arc is the same length but slides toward ER. IR looks “lost” — but only because ER gained the same amount. Nothing here is tight.

On average, a thrower’s dominant humerus sits about 12° more retroverted than the non‑dominant one. But that average hides enormous individual variation — measured torsion runs from roughly 12° of antetorsion to 46° of retrotorsion between people, and by up to 46° side‑to‑side within one person. Whiteley & Oceguera’s own conclusion: a clinician who applies the population average to an individual athlete “will, as a result, be wrong much more often than not.” Measure it. Don’t assume it.

Three lenses, three different verdicts

Clinicians commonly reach for one of three measures to decide whether a throwing shoulder’s rotation is a problem. They are not interchangeable — each answers a different question about the same two numbers.

GIRD
Glenohumeral Internal Rotation Deficit
IRuninvolved − IRinvolved
≥ 20° → flagged “at risk”

Flags any loss of involved‑arm IR, from any cause — soft tissue or bone. Doesn’t ask why the IR differs, only that it does.

TRROM
Total Rotational ROM difference
(IR+ER)uninvolved − (IR+ER)involved
≥ 5° → flagged “at risk”

Looks at the whole arc, not one end of it. Catches a genuinely smaller arc — but is blind to a shift that keeps the arc the same size, and doesn’t say which direction any real loss comes from.

GIRD‑torsion
GIRD adjusted for measured torsion
GIRD − torsion difference
≥ 10° (either direction) → flagged

Subtracts the part of GIRD that bony torsion already predicts, isolating what’s left for soft tissue to explain. Needs an actual torsion measurement to mean anything.

How often do they agree? Rarely.

In 162 professional baseball, handball and volleyball players, these three approaches flagged very different numbers of athletes as “at risk” — and mostly disagreed on which athletes.

What torsion actually looks like

Torsion is the twist along the humeral shaft between the humeral head at one end and the elbow’s flexion‑extension axis at the other. It varies enormously between people and is not fixed at birth — throwing itself appears to increase retrotorsion on the dominant side during growth.
